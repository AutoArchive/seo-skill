# Research Blog

## Purpose

Produce one useful long-form research article from question selection through
public verification. This single skill owns research, writing, article review,
and publication. Site-specific topics and editorial identity remain in the
consuming repository.

The public result must read like a genuine humanities or social-science article:
it poses a real question, advances one sustained thesis, interprets evidence,
engages competing explanations, states limitations, contributes an original
synthesis, and provides a complete reference list.

## Start with the point

Before research, write two sentences internally:

- **Thesis:** the concrete central claim the article will establish.
- **Reader use:** what a reader can understand, decide, translate, interpret, or
  do better after reading it.

Prefer a concrete thesis over an abstract formula. Name the actual distinction,
mechanism, historical change, interpretive dispute, or practical consequence.
The thesis and reader use appear clearly in the abstract and opening, guide every
main section, and return in the conclusion. A reader should always be able to
state what the article argues, why the argument matters, and how to use it.

A style revision must preserve the existing thesis, evidence, examples, nuance,
counterevidence, limitations, and practical value. Never trade substantive
content for a cleaner-sounding formula.

## Research depth

Use multiple rounds of public-web Deep Research and Sider Scholar. Search every
materially relevant language. Research involving Japanese concepts must include
Japanese-language queries and Japanese research or primary material.

A normal article uses at least 40 substantive sources:

- at least 20 academic sources, including peer-reviewed papers, preprints,
  conference or workshop papers, theses, dissertations, academic books, or book
  chapters;
- at least 20 primary, community, archival, institutional, lexicographic,
  contemporaneous-journalism, specialist-blog, creator, platform, or other
  first-hand sources.

The counts are floors for a normal article. Broader, disputed, historical, or
cross-language claims may require more. Search snippets, AI summaries, citation
lists, and pages that merely repeat another source serve discovery only.

Research proceeds in several rounds:

1. map definitions, history, major interpretations, relevant languages, and
   the closest existing article;
2. use Sider Scholar and public scholarly search to find academic literature;
3. collect primary, community, archival, platform, media, and specialist-blog
   material;
4. deep-read the sources that carry the thesis;
5. run a separate search for counterevidence, earlier cases, competing
   explanations, and communities that use the terms differently;
6. revise the thesis and scope;
7. verify every material claim and bibliography entry.

Keep claim-level citations close to the supported text. Clearly separate source
findings from the article's original synthesis.

## Humanities and social-science article form

The article normally contains:

1. a specific, searchable title that states the real question or finding;
2. an abstract summarizing the question, materials, thesis, contribution, and
   reader use;
3. keywords;
4. an opening that establishes the problem and states the thesis;
5. a literature, historical, or conceptual context section when the argument
   requires one;
6. a materials and methods section explaining the source corpus, languages,
   periods, platforms, comparison method, and source limits;
7. several sustained analytical sections, each advancing the central thesis
   through evidence and interpretation;
8. a serious counterevidence or alternative-explanation section;
9. a clearly identified original synthesis;
10. a limitations section;
11. a conclusion that directly answers the opening question and explains what
    the reader can now do with the result;
12. a complete reference list containing every cited source and only sources
    genuinely used.

Use the form flexibly, while preserving the intellectual functions above.
Headings should express substantive questions, distinctions, mechanisms, or
findings. The article should resemble a real humanities or social-science paper
adapted for public reading, rather than a glossary, FAQ, listicle, research log,
or sequence of source summaries.

## Write a rich article

The Chinese main text contains at least 5,000 Chinese characters, excluding front
matter and bibliography. Use the consuming site's strongest existing long-form
work as the local baseline for density, paragraph development, rhythm, and depth.
Preserve the context and reasoning that make the evidence meaningful.

A substantial section normally develops:

- concrete material, examples, texts, archives, or cases;
- historical, linguistic, social, technical, or literary context;
- relevant source interpretations;
- comparison with another case or explanation;
- counterevidence or boundary conditions;
- connection back to the central thesis;
- consequence or use for the reader.

Paragraphs should feel complete and naturally connected. Main analytical
paragraphs generally use several developed sentences and resemble the paragraph
length and cadence of the site's strongest prior work. Short paragraphs may mark
a turn or conclusion; repeated one- or two-sentence fragments usually signal that
evidence or reasoning has been compressed too far. Tables and lists may clarify a
comparison, while the main argument remains in developed prose.

Content richness has priority over superficial brevity. Preserve useful examples,
competing interpretations, mechanisms, limitations, and implications. A style
edit changes expression while retaining the thesis, evidence, nuance, and
practical value.

## Required article content

The finished article includes:

- a clear question and concrete central thesis;
- an explicit explanation of the article's intellectual or practical usefulness;
- developed evidence-led sections;
- in-text citations;
- serious counterevidence or alternative explanations;
- limitations and evidence boundaries;
- an original, clearly identified synthesis or insight;
- a conclusion that returns to the thesis and reader use;
- a complete bibliography in a consistent humanities or social-science citation
  style.

## Collaborative affirmative language

Write as an informed collaborator who brings the reader through the materials and
shows how the judgment forms. Use calm, direct, affirmative sentences. Let
examples and evidence create transitions instead of repeatedly correcting an
imagined opponent.

Strictly avoid negative and defensive constructions in author-written prose.
This includes correction-first sentences, denial-centered contrasts,
anticipatory rebuttals, disclaimers, self-protective caveats, and formulae such
as “不是……而是……”, “并非”, “并不是”, “不只是”, “不仅仅是”,
“这并不意味着”, “不能简单地说”, “无法证明”, “本文无意”,
“需要澄清”, and equivalent patterns in other languages.

Rewrite these constructions as direct positive statements of:

- what the evidence shows;
- which scope the claim covers;
- how two concepts differ;
- what chronology is verified;
- what relationship exists;
- how confident the conclusion is;
- which question remains open.

Run two separate audits before publication:

1. **Literal negation scan:** search every author-written title, description,
   abstract, heading, paragraph, caption, callout, synthesis, limitations section,
   and conclusion for negative markers and negative-contrast patterns.
2. **Defensive semantic review:** read the complete article for sentences whose
   function is denial, correction, anticipated objection, disclaimer, or
   self-defense, even when a literal marker is absent.

Rewrite every detected case. Source-accurate quotations, official titles, proper
names, code, and bibliography entries retain their original wording.

A user-specified research question or exact title may retain one necessary logical
negation when that wording is itself the subject being answered. This narrow
exception applies only to the exact question or title. The abstract and body state
the thesis through direct affirmative distinctions and evidence.

This language rule never authorizes deleting evidence, counterarguments,
limitations, qualifications, examples, explanation, or the article's central
thesis. Express every boundary positively and fully.

## Review

Review the complete article from the beginning and answer these questions:

1. Can a reader state the concrete thesis after the abstract and opening?
2. Is the article's practical or intellectual use explicit?
3. Does the structure perform the functions of a real humanities or
   social-science article?
4. Does every section advance the thesis through developed evidence and
   interpretation?
5. Does the article match or exceed the site's established long-form richness,
   paragraph length, flow, and depth?
6. Are at least 40 substantive sources represented, including at least 20
   academic and 20 primary, community, archival, institutional, media, creator,
   platform, or specialist sources?
7. Were multiple public-web research rounds and Sider Scholar used, with Japanese
   research for Japanese concepts?
8. Are in-text citations and the complete reference list accurate and consistent?
9. Are counterevidence, limitations, and original synthesis represented fairly?
10. Does every material factual claim have adequate support?
11. Did both the literal negation scan and defensive semantic review pass?
12. Does the prose remain collaborative, natural, affirmative, and rich?

Revise until every answer is yes.

## Delivery

Use `$deliver-github-pr` for the repository change. Include the article, closely
related editorial updates, and the minimum required site records. Run the site's
authoritative checks, wait for complete green CI, review the final diff and
rendered article from scratch, squash-merge, verify the exact production provider
deployment, and confirm the canonical public article.

## Completion

The work is complete when the article has a concrete thesis and reader use, a
genuine humanities or social-science structure, at least 5,000 Chinese main-text
characters, at least 40 substantive sources in the required composition, rich and
well-supported prose, in-text citations, a complete reference list,
counterevidence, limitations, original synthesis, passed literal and semantic
language audits, a clean final review, a merged pull request, and verified
production output on the canonical site.
